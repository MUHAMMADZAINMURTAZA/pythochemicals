What are Phytochemicals?

Phytochemicals (Greek: phyton = plant) are biologically active naturally occurring chemical compounds found in plants. They protect plants against disease, predation, and environmental stress — and many have significant pharmacological activity in humans.

Key definition: Secondary metabolites of plants that are not essential for basic plant growth but serve ecological and defensive roles — and are the basis of many drugs.

Primary vs Secondary Metabolites

Primary Metabolites
Essential for life — carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids. Universally present in all cells.
Secondary Metabolites (Phytochemicals)
Not essential for basic survival. Found in specific plant species. Include alkaloids, glycosides, terpenes, flavonoids etc.

Importance in Pharmacy

  • About 25% of modern drugs are derived directly from plants or synthesized based on plant phytochemicals
  • Basis of traditional medicine systems (Ayurveda, Unani, TCM)
  • Used as lead compounds for drug discovery
  • Used as excipients (binders, coating agents) in pharmaceuticals
  • Quality control markers for standardization of herbal drugs

Alkaloids

Alkaloids are nitrogen-containing organic compounds of plant origin that are basic in nature (contain nitrogen in heterocyclic ring), have complex molecular structure, and show marked physiological activity.

Definition by Pelletier (1983): A cyclic organic compound containing nitrogen in a negative oxidation state which is of limited distribution among living organisms.

General Properties

  • Mostly basic in nature (due to lone pair of electrons on N)
  • Bitter taste, white crystalline solids (mostly)
  • Sparingly soluble in water; soluble in organic solvents
  • Alkaloid salts are water soluble and used in medicine
  • Optically active (laevorotatory mostly)
  • React with alkaloidal reagents (precipitate tests)

Biosynthetic Classification

  • From ornithine: Pyrrolizidine, Tropane alkaloids (Atropine)
  • From lysine: Piperidine, Quinolizidine alkaloids (Sparteine)
  • From tyrosine: Isoquinoline alkaloids (Morphine)
  • From tryptophan: Indole alkaloids (Ergotamine, Reserpine)
  • From anthranilic acid: Quinazoline alkaloids
  • From histidine: Imidazole alkaloids (Pilocarpine)
  • From purines: Purine alkaloids (Caffeine)

Important Individual Alkaloids

Morphine (Opium alkaloid)

  • Source: Papaver somniferum (Opium poppy), Family: Papaveraceae
  • Type: Isoquinoline alkaloid (Phenanthrene nucleus)
  • Use: Narcotic analgesic, severe pain, pre-anesthetic medication
  • Chemical test: Marquis test (H₂SO₄ + formaldehyde → purple/violet color)

Quinine

  • Source: Cinchona bark (Cinchona succirubra), Family: Rubiaceae
  • Type: Quinoline alkaloid
  • Use: Antimalarial, Antipyretic, Bitter tonic, Antiarrhythmic
  • Chemical test: Thalleioquin test — green color with bromine water + ammonia

Atropine

  • Source: Atropa belladonna (Deadly nightshade), Family: Solanaceae
  • Type: Tropane alkaloid
  • Use: Anticholinergic, Mydriatic, Pre-anesthetic, Antispasmodic
  • Chemical test: Vitali-Morin test — purple color with fuming nitric acid + KOH

Ergotamine

  • Source: Claviceps purpurea (ergot fungus on rye), Family: Clavicipitaceae
  • Type: Indole alkaloid (Ergoline nucleus)
  • Use: Migraine, Uterine stimulant, Hemostatic

Reserpine

  • Source: Rauwolfia serpentina (Indian snakeroot), Family: Apocynaceae
  • Type: Indole alkaloid
  • Use: Antihypertensive, Antipsychotic (depletes catecholamines)

Vinca Alkaloids (Vinblastine & Vincristine)

  • Source: Catharanthus roseus (Vinca rosea), Family: Apocynaceae
  • Type: Bis-indole alkaloid
  • Use: Anticancer — inhibit tubulin polymerization (mitotic spindle poison)

Glycosides

Glycosides are organic compounds in which a sugar (glycone) is bonded to a non-sugar moiety (aglycone/genin) through a glycosidic linkage (usually O-glycosidic bond). On hydrolysis (acid, alkali, or enzyme), they yield sugar + aglycone.

Glycoside = Glycone (sugar) + Aglycone (non-sugar/genin)
The pharmacological activity resides mainly in the aglycone portion; the sugar portion influences solubility, absorption, and potency.

General Properties

  • Colorless crystalline solids, bitter taste
  • Optically active (usually laevorotatory)
  • Hydrolyzed by acids, alkalis, or specific enzymes (emulsin, synaptase)
  • Water soluble due to sugar component

Classification of Glycosides

Based on Nature of Glycosidic Bond

  • O-glycosides: Most common — sugar linked to aglycone via oxygen
  • S-glycosides (Thioglycosides): Linked via sulfur — e.g., Sinigrin (mustard)
  • N-glycosides: Linked via nitrogen — e.g., Nucleosides
  • C-glycosides: Direct C–C bond — e.g., Barbaloin (aloe)

Cardiac Glycosides — Most Important

Sources:

  • Digitalis purpurea → Digitoxin, Digitalinum verum (Family: Scrophulariaceae)
  • Digitalis lanata → Digoxin, Lanatosides (most used clinically)
  • Strophanthus kombe → Ouabain (G-strophanthin)
  • Convallaria majalis → Convallotoxin
  • Bufo toad → Bufadienolides (animal source)

Mechanism of Action

Inhibit Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump → ↑ intracellular Na⁺ → ↑ intracellular Ca²⁺ (via Na/Ca exchanger) → positive inotropic effect (stronger heart contraction)

Chemical Tests

  • Keller-Kiliani test: Digitoxose (deoxy sugar) — blue/green ring at junction with H₂SO₄ + FeCl₃
  • Legal's test: Lactone ring — red color with Na-nitroprusside + NaOH
  • Kedde's test: Unsaturated lactone — violet color with 3,5-dinitrobenzoic acid + KOH
  • Liebermann-Burchard test: Steroidal nucleus — green color
  • Raymond test: Red-violet color with m-dinitrobenzene + KOH

Saponins

Saponins are glycosides that produce persistent foam when shaken with water (like soap). They lower surface tension of water.

Types

  • Triterpenoid saponins: Glycyrrhizin (liquorice), Escin (horse chestnut)
  • Steroidal saponins: Diosgenin (Dioscorea sp.) — used for synthesis of steroidal drugs (cortisone, contraceptives)

Properties

  • Produce stable froth/foam with water
  • Hemolytic (rupture RBCs) — except soya saponins
  • Highly toxic if injected IV but relatively non-toxic orally
  • Precipitate with cholesterol

Uses

  • Expectorants (stimulate respiratory mucus secretion)
  • Diuretics
  • Source for steroid hormone synthesis (Diosgenin)
  • Adjuvants in vaccines

Terpenes & Terpenoids

Terpenes are the largest and most diverse class of natural products. They are hydrocarbons built from isoprene (C₅H₈) units following the "Isoprene Rule" (Wallach's rule). Terpenoids are oxygenated derivatives of terpenes.

Isoprene unit: CH₂=C(CH₃)–CH=CH₂ (C₅H₈)
All terpenes are multiples of isoprene units (C₅ rule).

Important Terpenes

Menthol (Monoterpene)

  • Source: Mentha piperita (Peppermint), Family: Lamiaceae
  • Type: Cyclic monoterpene alcohol
  • Use: Counterirritant, Carminative, Nasal decongestant, Flavoring agent, Antiseptic
  • Activates TRPM8 cold receptors — produces cooling sensation

Camphor (Monoterpene)

  • Source: Cinnamomum camphora, Family: Lauraceae
  • Type: Bicyclic monoterpene ketone
  • Use: Counterirritant, Antipruritic, Mild antiseptic, Expectorant

Artemisinin (Sesquiterpene)

  • Source: Artemisia annua (Sweet wormwood), Family: Asteraceae
  • Type: Sesquiterpene lactone with endoperoxide bridge
  • Use: Most effective antimalarial, especially for drug-resistant P. falciparum
  • Acts by generating free radicals that damage parasite proteins

Paclitaxel/Taxol (Diterpene)

  • Source: Taxus brevifolia (Pacific yew), Family: Taxaceae
  • Type: Diterpene
  • Use: Anticancer — ovarian, breast, lung cancer
  • MOA: Stabilizes microtubules → prevents mitosis (opposite to vinca alkaloids)

β-Carotene (Tetraterpene)

  • Source: Carrots, tomatoes, plant pigments
  • Type: Tetraterpene (carotenoid)
  • Use: Pro-vitamin A, Antioxidant, prevents xerophthalmia

Phenolic Compounds & Flavonoids

Phenolic compounds contain one or more hydroxyl groups (–OH) attached to an aromatic benzene ring. They are the most abundant secondary metabolites in plants. Flavonoids are a subclass of polyphenols with a C₆-C₃-C₆ (diphenylpropane) skeleton.

Flavonoids

Basic structure: Two phenyl rings (A and B) connected by a 3-carbon chain forming a pyran ring. General formula: C₆-C₃-C₆

Pharmacological Activities of Flavonoids

  • Antioxidant: Scavenge free radicals, chelate metal ions
  • Anti-inflammatory: Inhibit COX, LOX enzymes
  • Anticancer: Induce apoptosis, inhibit angiogenesis
  • Cardiovascular: Reduce platelet aggregation, strengthen capillaries
  • Antimicrobial: Disrupt cell membranes of microbes
  • Antiviral: Quercetin active against influenza, HIV

Hesperidin

  • Source: Citrus species (orange peel)

Tannins

Tannins are complex polyphenolic compounds with molecular weight 500–3000 Da that precipitate proteins. The term "tannin" comes from their ability to convert (tan) animal hides into leather.

Definition: Water-soluble polyphenolic compounds that precipitate proteins, alkaloids, and gelatin from solution, and produce a blue-black or green-black color with iron salts.

Classification of Tannins

1. Hydrolysable Tannins
Hydrolyzed by acid, alkali, or enzymes into sugars + phenolic acids.

Gallotannins: Yield gallic acid (e.g., Tannic acid from galls, Myrabolan)
Ellagitannins: Yield ellagic acid (e.g., Pomegranate rind)
2. Condensed Tannins (Proanthocyanidins)
Not easily hydrolyzed; on acid treatment produce anthocyanidins (red color). Formed by condensation of flavan-3-ols.

Examples: Catechu (from Acacia catechu), Gambir

Pharmacological Uses of Tannins

  • Astringent: Precipitate proteins on mucous membranes → protective film → reduces inflammation/irritation
  • Hemostatic: Constrict small blood vessels
  • Antidiarrheal: Reduce intestinal secretion
  • Antimicrobial: Precipitate bacterial proteins
  • Antioxidant
  • Antidote: Precipitate alkaloids and heavy metals in poisoning

Chemical Tests for Tannins

  • FeCl₃ test → Blue-black (hydrolysable) or Green-black (condensed) color
  • Gelatin test → White precipitate
  • Lead acetate test → White precipitate
  • Bromine water → Decolorization

Important Tannin-containing Drugs

  • Catechu: Acacia catechu — Astringent for mouth ulcers
  • Tannic acid: From oak galls — Antidote for alkaloid poisoning
  • Witch hazel: Hamamelis virginiana — Hemorrhoids, skin care
  • Pomegranate rind: Anthelmintic, astringent

Resins

Resins are amorphous solid or semi-solid oxidized terpene products formed in specialized secretory cells. They are insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents (alcohol, ether).

Classification

  • Resins: Pure — e.g., Colophony (rosin from turpentine), Jalap resin
  • Oleoresins: Resin + volatile oil — e.g., Turpentine, Ginger, Capsicum
  • Oleo-gum-resins: Resin + gum + volatile oil — e.g., Asafoetida, Myrrh, Galbanum
  • Balsams: Resins with aromatic acids (benzoic/cinnamic acid) — e.g., Benzoin, Peru balsam, Tolu balsam
  • Glycoresins: Resin with sugar — e.g., Jalap, Scammony, Podophyllum

Pharmaceutical Uses of Resins

  • Colophony → Sticking plasters, zinc ointment base
  • Balsam of Peru → Wound healing, scabies
  • Benzoin → Expectorant (Friar's balsam), antiseptic
  • Podophyllum resin → Condylomata acuminata treatment, anticancer (Etoposide)
  • Asafoetida → Carminative, antispasmodic

Volatile (Essential) Oils

Volatile oils (ethereal oils/essential oils) are odoriferous, volatile liquid mixtures of terpenes and other aromatic compounds obtained from plant materials. They evaporate at room temperature (hence "volatile").

Key difference from fixed oils: Volatile oils evaporate readily, leave no permanent grease stain on paper, and are not glycerol esters.

Chemical Composition

  • Terpenes: Monoterpenes (major) — limonene, pinene, cymene
  • Oxygenated compounds: Alcohols (menthol), aldehydes (citral), ketones (camphor), esters (linalyl acetate), phenols (thymol, eugenol), oxides (eucalyptol/1,8-cineole)
  • Sulfur/Nitrogen compounds: Sinigrin (mustard), garlic compounds

Fixed Oils (Fatty Oils)

Fixed oils are non-volatile, oily liquids or solids composed mainly of glycerides (esters of glycerol + fatty acids). They leave a permanent grease stain on paper and do NOT evaporate.

Classification

  • Drying oils: High in polyunsaturated fatty acids — dry on exposure to air (linseed, tung oil)
  • Semi-drying oils: Partially dry — sunflower, cottonseed, sesame oil
  • Non-drying oils: Do not dry — castor oil (ricinoleic acid), olive oil (oleic acid), almond oil

Cyanogenic Glycosides & Mustard Oil Glycosides

Cyanogenic Glycosides

  • Release HCN (hydrocyanic acid) on hydrolysis by β-glucosidase enzyme
  • Amygdalin: Prunus amygdalus (bitter almond), apricot seeds — antitussive; controversial as "Laetrile" in cancer
  • Prunasin: Prunus species
  • HCN inhibits cytochrome c oxidase → cellular asphyxia → toxic in high doses

Glucosinolates (Isothiocyanate Glycosides)

  • Release isothiocyanates + glucose + sulfate on hydrolysis by myrosinase
  • Sinigrin: Black mustard (Brassica nigra) → allyl isothiocyanate (counterirritant)
  • Sinalbin: White mustard (Sinapis alba) → p-hydroxybenzyl isothiocyanate (milder)
  • Used externally as rubefacient/counterirritant (mustard plasters)

Predicted Viva Questions & Model Answers

Q: What are phytochemicals? How are they different from primary metabolites?
Phytochemicals are secondary metabolites of plants — biologically active compounds not essential for basic plant survival but providing protection and ecological functions. Unlike primary metabolites (carbohydrates, proteins, lipids) which are universal and essential for all cells, secondary metabolites are species-specific and serve roles like defense against herbivores, UV protection, and attraction of pollinators. Examples: alkaloids, glycosides, terpenes, flavonoids.
Q: What is the difference between an alkaloid and a glycoside?
Alkaloids: N-containing, basic compounds, heterocyclic ring, no sugar component. Examples: morphine, quinine. Glycosides: contain sugar (glycone) + non-sugar (aglycone) linked by glycosidic bond. No N in ring (usually). Examples: digoxin, sennosides. Key test: alkaloids precipitate with Mayer's/Dragendorff's; glycosides undergo hydrolysis to sugar + aglycone.
Q: Explain the mechanism of action of cardiac glycosides.
Cardiac glycosides (e.g., digoxin) inhibit Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump on cardiac cell membranes. This causes intracellular Na⁺ accumulation. The Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger then pumps less Ca²⁺ out, increasing intracellular Ca²⁺. Higher Ca²⁺ during systole causes stronger cardiac contraction (positive inotropic effect). They also slow heart rate (negative chronotropic) via vagal stimulation.
Q: What is the isoprene rule? Give examples of each class of terpenes.
Proposed by Wallach — all terpenes are built from C₅ isoprene units (2-methylbuta-1,3-diene). Examples: Monoterpenes (C₁₀, 2 units) = menthol, camphor; Sesquiterpenes (C₁₅, 3 units) = artemisinin; Diterpenes (C₂₀, 4 units) = taxol; Triterpenes (C₃₀, 6 units) = squalene; Tetraterpenes (C₄₀, 8 units) = β-carotene.
Q: What are saponins? How are they classified? Give uses.
Saponins are glycosides that produce persistent froth when shaken with water, due to their soap-like (amphiphilic) structure. Classified as: (1) Triterpenoid saponins — glycyrrhizin from liquorice, (2) Steroidal saponins — diosgenin from Dioscorea (used to synthesize cortisone, contraceptives). Properties: hemolytic, foam-forming, precipitate cholesterol. Uses: expectorants, diuretics, vaccine adjuvants, steroid synthesis precursors.
Q: Differentiate between hydrolysable and condensed tannins.
Hydrolysable tannins: hydrolyzed by acid/alkali/enzyme into sugars + gallic acid (gallotannins) or ellagic acid (ellagitannins). FeCl₃ gives blue-black. Example: tannic acid. Condensed tannins: resistant to hydrolysis; on acid treatment give red phlobaphenes (anthocyanidins). FeCl₃ gives green-black. Example: catechu (catechins/proanthocyanidins). Higher MW than hydrolysable.
Q: What are the differences between volatile oils and fixed oils?
Volatile oils: evaporate at room temp, no grease stain, mainly terpenes/oxygenated compounds, odoriferous, used for antiseptic/carminative/expectorant effects. Fixed oils: non-volatile, glycerol esters of fatty acids, leave permanent grease stain, used as emollients/vehicles/laxatives. Test: place on filter paper — volatile oil evaporates without stain, fixed oil leaves permanent stain.
Q: Name alkaloid identification tests and what they detect.
General alkaloid precipitant tests — Mayer's (cream ppt), Dragendorff's (orange-red ppt), Wagner's (brown ppt), Hager's (yellow ppt). Specific tests: Marquis test (morphine → purple), Vitali-Morin (atropine → purple with fuming HNO₃+KOH), Thalleioquin (quinine → green). These tests exploit the basic nitrogen's ability to form insoluble complexes with heavy metal reagents.
Q: What is Shinoda test? For which class of compounds is it used?
Shinoda test is used to identify flavonoids. A small amount of magnesium ribbon (or powder) is added to the plant extract, followed by concentrated HCl. Flavonoids (flavones, flavonols, flavanones) give a pink to red color due to reduction by nascent hydrogen. Absence of color suggests absence of flavonoids. Isoflavones give a pale yellow to no color.
Q: What is the pharmacological importance of Artemisinin?
Artemisinin is a sesquiterpene lactone from Artemisia annua. It contains an endoperoxide bridge (–O–O–) which is responsible for activity. It reacts with heme iron in malaria parasites to form free radicals that alkylate and damage parasite proteins. It is the most potent antimalarial especially against drug-resistant Plasmodium falciparum. Nobel Prize in Physiology (2015) awarded to Tu Youyou for its discovery. Used as Artemisinin Combination Therapy (ACT).
Q: What are resins? Classify them with examples.
Resins are amorphous, solid/semi-solid oxidized terpene secretions insoluble in water but soluble in alcohol/ether. Classified as: (1) Resins — colophony/jalap resin; (2) Oleoresins (resin+volatile oil) — turpentine/ginger; (3) Oleo-gum-resins (resin+gum+volatile oil) — asafoetida/myrrh; (4) Balsams (resin+benzoic/cinnamic acid) — benzoin/peru balsam; (5) Glycoresins (resin+sugar) — jalap/podophyllum.
Q: Which phytochemical is the precursor for steroid hormone synthesis?
Diosgenin — a steroidal saponin aglycone obtained from Dioscorea species (wild yam). It is a key raw material (starting material) for the partial synthesis of corticosteroids (cortisone), progestogens, androgens, and estrogens including oral contraceptives. Stigmasterol (from soybean) is also used similarly.

Chemical Tests Quick Recall

  • Mayer's/Dragendorff's/Wagner's → Alkaloids (precipitate)
  • Keller-Kiliani → Deoxy sugars in cardiac glycosides (blue ring)
  • Legal/Kedde/Raymond → Lactone ring of cardiac glycosides
  • Shinoda test → Flavonoids (Mg + HCl → pink/red)
  • FeCl₃ → Tannins (blue-black/green-black) & phenolics
  • Foam test → Saponins
  • Liebermann-Burchard → Steroids/Triterpenes (green)
